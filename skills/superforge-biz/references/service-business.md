# Service Business — when the thing you sell is your capacity

Everything else in this skill assumes a product: build once, sell many times,
and the interesting question is pricing. A service business — 受託, an agency, a
retainer, consulting — inverts that. It sells hours, and hours do not scale.
Almost every failure in this shape traces back to running it as though it were a
product business, or refusing to admit it is a service business at all.

This matters even for people who intend to build products, because a service
business is the most common way an indie maker funds one. Run knowingly, it buys
the runway. Run unknowingly, it consumes the time the product was supposed to
get.

---

## 1. The ceiling exists, and you should compute it first

> **上限 = 稼働可能時間 × 単価 × 稼働率**

A product's revenue ceiling is the market. A service's ceiling is **arithmetic**,
and it is much lower than people assume because two of the three terms are
smaller than they feel:

- **稼働可能時間** is not your working hours. Sales, proposals, invoicing,
  scoping, and the meeting about the meeting are all unbillable. Assume **50–70%
  billable at best**, and lower while you are still finding clients.
- **稼働率** is the fraction of that time actually sold. Gaps between projects
  are structural, not a personal failure.

Compute the number before taking the work:

```
週30時間 × 60%請求可能 = 週18時間
週18時間 × $150 × 45週 = 約$121,500/年
```

That is the ceiling at that rate — not a target, a **wall**. Everything above it
requires changing a term, and there are only four ways to do that:

| Lever | What it costs |
|---|---|
| **単価を上げる** | Requires proof, a narrower specialty, or a better-funded buyer. The only lever with no structural downside |
| **稼働率を上げる** | Requires a pipeline, which is unbillable work. Diminishing, and above ~80% it eats the product time entirely |
| **人を増やす** | You stop delivering and start managing. A different job, and the margin per person is thin until there are several |
| **成果物を再利用する** | The bridge to a product business — see §6 |

**Say the ceiling out loud when advising.** A plan to reach a revenue number
that the arithmetic forbids is not ambitious, it is a plan that fails on
schedule, and the person deserves to see the equation before they sign.

---

## 2. The scope *is* the product

For a product, the deliverable is the software. For a service, the deliverable
is **the sentence describing what will be delivered.** Everything about the
project's profitability is decided when that sentence is written, before any
work happens.

A scope is well-formed when it names all four:

1. **成果物** — what exists at the end, listed as artifacts. "Landing page
   design" is not an artifact; "1ページ分のデザイン（デスクトップ＋モバイル）、
   Figmaファイルで納品" is
2. **回数** — how many rounds of revision are included, as a number
3. **含まれないもの** — stated explicitly, in the same document. This is the
   section people skip and the one that pays
4. **終わりの条件** — what event ends the engagement. Delivery? Acceptance?
   A date? Silence for two weeks?

The fourth is the most commonly missing and the most expensive. **A project with
no defined end does not end** — it decays into unpaid maintenance that is
socially impossible to refuse.

**Write the excluded list before the included list.** It is the faster way to
find the ambiguities, and it is far easier to add something later than to
retract it.

---

## 3. Scope creep is the single largest loss driver

Not underpricing. Not late payment. **Creep** — because it is invisible per
instance and cumulative in effect. Each request is small, reasonable, and
delivered "while we're in there," and the tenth one has halved the effective
rate on a project that still looks successful.

Two things prevent it, and both are mechanical rather than a matter of firmness:

**① Make every out-of-scope request visible at the moment it arrives.** Not
refused — **priced**. The response is always the same shape:

> 「それは今回のスコープ外ですが、対応できます。追加で X 時間 / $Y、納期は Z
> 日後ろ倒しになります。進めますか？」

This is not confrontational. It converts an invisible cost into a decision the
client is entitled to make. Most say yes, some say no, and either answer is
better than absorbing it silently.

**② Log every request, including the ones you absorb.** A running list turns
"the project felt heavy" into "seven unbilled changes, 11 hours." Without the
log, the next quote repeats the same underpricing, because the loss was never
measured.

**The one that is never worth absorbing quietly:** a change to the *definition*
of the deliverable, as opposed to a detail within it. Restate the scope in
writing before continuing, every time.

## 4. Project or retainer — pick by what the client actually needs

| | **案件 (project)** | **顧問 / 保守 (retainer)** |
|---|---|---|
| Fits when | The need has a clear end | The need recurs, or is availability itself |
| Revenue | Lumpy — feast and famine | Predictable, which is its entire value |
| Scope risk | High: fixed price, discovered work | Different: unbounded availability |
| Ends | On delivery | Never, until cancelled — plan for it |
| Client's real ask | 「これを作ってほしい」 | 「困ったときに人がいてほしい」 |

**A retainer must be bounded by something**, or it becomes an on-call obligation
priced as a small monthly fee. Bound it by hours per month, by response time, by
a named scope of systems, or by a number of requests — but bound it by
something, and state what happens when the bound is hit.

**The trap is a retainer sold as insurance.** If most months are quiet, the
client eventually asks what they are paying for; if most months are busy, you
are underwater. Price it as **reserved capacity**, and say so — the client is
buying the guarantee that someone is available, which stays true in a quiet
month.

**The strongest sequence:** a small paid project first, a retainer after. It
prices the relationship on evidence instead of estimate, and both sides get to
find out whether they can work together at low cost.

## 5. Concentration is the risk that ends service businesses

> **一社が売上の50%を超えたら、それは顧客ではなく雇用主です。**

And an employer without notice, severance, or the right to say no.

| Largest client's share | State |
|---|---|
| **> 50%** | You are structurally an employee. Their reorganisation is your quarter |
| **30–50%** | Losing them is a crisis. Diversify actively, starting now |
| **< 25%** | Losing them hurts and survives |

Three practical rules:

- **Do the pipeline work while you are busy**, not when the busy work ends.
  Sales during a gap is late by however long the sales cycle is, and the gap is
  exactly as long as that cycle
- **Watch payment behaviour as a leading signal.** Payment terms slipping is the
  earliest reliable indicator that a client relationship is ending or that they
  are in trouble — well before any conversation about it
- **The best client is not the largest.** Rank by *rate × ease*, not by revenue.
  The biggest account is frequently the worst-paying one per hour once the
  meetings are counted

## 6. When pricing moves off time — and when it must not

Hourly and daily rates are honest, easy to justify, and they permanently cap
income at §1's ceiling. They also **pay you for being slow**, which is a strange
thing to build a career on.

Move off time when **all three** hold:

1. You have done this specific work before, enough to estimate it within ~25%
2. The outcome is nameable and the client can tell whether they got it
3. The scope is closed (§2) — no undefined edges

Then price against the client's number rather than yours. A checkout flow whose
conversion improvement is worth $200k/year is not a "40-hour job"; the hours are
your cost structure and have nothing to do with its value. `references/
value-pitch.md` is the mechanism for getting to that number, and it applies
here more directly than anywhere else in this skill.

**Stay on time-based pricing when:** the work is genuinely exploratory, the
client cannot articulate what "done" means, it is the first project together, or
the estimate rests on someone else's system you have not seen. Fixed price on
undiscovered work is how a profitable quarter becomes an unpaid one.

**Never price on outcomes you do not control.** Revenue share on a launch whose
marketing you do not run, or a fee contingent on approval by a third party, is a
lottery ticket described as a contract.

### The bridge out

Every repeated engagement contains a reusable core — a starting template, a
checklist, an internal tool, a piece of infrastructure. **Extract it
deliberately after the second time**, not the fifth. That artifact is what turns
the ceiling in §1 from a wall into a slope, and it is the most common honest
route from a service business into a product one.

---

## 7. Before quoting

- [ ] The ceiling (§1) is computed, and the plan lives underneath it
- [ ] The scope names artifacts, revision count, exclusions, and an end condition
- [ ] The excluded list was written first
- [ ] Payment terms: deposit up front, and what happens if an invoice goes unpaid
- [ ] Project or retainer chosen by the client's actual need (§4), and a retainer
      has a stated bound
- [ ] If fixed-price: all three conditions in §6 hold. If any fails, price by time
- [ ] Concentration checked (§5) — what share of revenue this client becomes
- [ ] Anything carrying the client's name is fact-checked before delivery
      (`superforge-verify/references/evidence.md` §5)
